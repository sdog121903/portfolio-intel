# Everyday analogies (use one, then add the precise version)

An analogy opens the door; the precise sentence after it stops the analogy from misleading.

| Idea | Analogy | Then add the precise version |
|---|---|---|
| What a stock price is | A ticket to a share of all the company's future profits. | Price = earnings per share x the multiple investors are willing to pay (P/E). |
| Why prices move on news | A weather forecast: people react to the *change* in the forecast, not to the weather itself. | Prices move when new information changes expected future profits or the risk around them. |
| Expectations ("priced in") | A student expected to get an A who gets an A-: good grade, disappointed parents. | If results beat the consensus by less than investors privately hoped, the stock can fall on "good" news. |
| Guidance | A restaurant telling you how many tables are already booked for next month. | Management's own forecast, based on orders and contracts outsiders cannot see. |
| Multiple (P/E) | How many years of today's profit a buyer pays upfront for a business. | A P/E of 40 means paying $40 for each $1 of yearly profit; high multiples assume fast growth. |
| Multiple compression | The same house selling for less because buyers became more cautious, not because the house changed. | The price falls while earnings hold steady: the P/E shrinks. |
| Interest rates and valuations | $100 promised in 10 years is worth less today when a bank pays you 5% than when it pays 1%. | Higher rates lower the present value of far-off profits, hitting fast-growing companies hardest. |
| Recurring revenue | Gym memberships versus one-off concert tickets. | Subscriptions repeat unless cancelled, so future revenue is more predictable. |
| Backlog | A bakery's book of pre-orders for wedding cakes. | Signed work not yet delivered or billed; it turns into revenue over the coming months. |
| Book-to-bill | Cakes ordered this month versus cakes delivered. | Above 1.0 means orders arrive faster than sales are recognised. |
| Gross margin | A lemonade stand: price of a cup minus the lemons, sugar and cup. | (Revenue - cost of goods sold) / revenue. |
| Operating leverage | A cinema: the building and staff cost the same whether 50 or 500 people come. | When costs are mostly fixed, extra sales fall straight to profit, and lost sales hurt profit fast. |
| Dilution | The same pizza cut into more slices. | New shares mean each existing share owns a smaller piece, unless the new money grows the business. |
| Buyback | The company buys slices back and throws them away. | Fewer shares outstanding, so each remaining share owns more. |
| CPU vs GPU | One brilliant professor solving problems one by one, versus thousands of students each doing simple sums at the same time. | GPUs run many small calculations in parallel, which is exactly what training and running AI models needs. |
| Data center | A warehouse full of computers that companies rent over the internet. | "The cloud" is these buildings; AI needs ones packed with GPUs, power and cooling. |
| Hyperscaler | The few landlords who own most of the biggest computer warehouses. | Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta and Oracle; their spending plans drive demand for chips, cables and construction. |
| Optical transceiver | A translator that turns electrical signals into flashes of laser light and back, sending them down a glass fibre. | Light carries data farther and faster than copper; data centers use millions of these links. |
| Active electrical cable | A long garden hose with small pumps built in, so the pressure stays strong to the end. | A copper cable with chips at each end that clean and boost the signal so it works at high speed over longer distances. |
| SerDes | A post office that packs many letters into one fast truck and unpacks them at the other end. | Serializer/deserializer: the circuit that turns many parallel data lines into one very fast stream and back. |
| Capex cycle | Farmers buying tractors: in good years everyone orders, in bad years nobody does. | Capital-spending booms create sharp rises in orders for suppliers, and sharp falls when they end. |
| Beta | A speedboat versus a cruise ship on the same waves. | A beta of 1.5 means the stock has tended to move about 1.5% when the market moved 1%. |
| Volatility | How bumpy the road is, not which direction it goes. | The typical size of daily price swings, scaled to a year. |
| Drawdown | How far you have fallen from the top of the hill you climbed. | The % decline from the highest previous price. |
| Moving average | Your average mood over the last 50 days compared with your mood today. | The average closing price over N days; price above it means recent prices are higher than the period's norm. |
| RSI | A runner's breathing after a sprint. | A 0-100 gauge of recent gains versus losses; above 70 means it rose fast, which is not a reason by itself for it to stop. |
| Correlation | Umbrellas and raincoats sell on the same days; umbrellas and sunscreen do not. | From -1 to +1: how much two prices move together. High correlation means less diversification. |
| Concentration | All your eggs in one basket, even if they look like different eggs. | Five stocks driven by one theme (AI data centers) behave more like one big bet. |
| Market vs limit order | Buying at a market stall at whatever price is on the sign, versus saying "I'll pay up to $5, no more". | Market orders fill now at the current price; limit orders fill only at your price or better. |
| 10b5-1 plan | Setting up an automatic savings transfer months in advance. | A pre-scheduled trading plan that lets insiders sell without being accused of using secret information. |
| Moat | The water around a castle that keeps attackers out. | A durable advantage (technology, switching costs, scale) that protects profits from competitors. |
| Export controls | A country deciding which tools its shops may sell to which foreign customers. | Government rules limiting the sale of advanced technology (such as AI chips) to certain countries. |
