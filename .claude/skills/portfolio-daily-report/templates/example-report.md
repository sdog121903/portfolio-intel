**How this works** Example Robotics sells robot arms that pick items off warehouse shelves. A
customer buys the robots once (hardware) and then pays a yearly fee for the software that runs
them, a bit like buying a printer and then paying for ink. When a big customer delays a
hardware order, this year's sales fall, but the software fees for robots already installed keep
coming, which is why the company says recurring revenue (the predictable, repeating part) is
unchanged at about $600 million a year. It does not disclose how many robots it ships per
quarter, so we cannot check the size of the delay directly.

2. Investors had been paying a high price for growth; slower growth can shrink that price further even if profits hold up.
3. Management cut guidance only three months after raising it, which makes its forecasts look less reliable.

## Lesson of the day

**Why can a stock fall 8% when the company is still growing?**
A share price is not a reward for last year; it is the market's best guess of all the profits a
company will make in the future. Yesterday, Example Robotics still expected to grow 12% this year.
But investors had been expecting about 20%, and the price already included that hope (it was
"priced in"). When the company itself lowered the forecast, investors recalculated the future
profits with the smaller number, so the price fell. Mini-example: if investors were paying $30
for every $1 of expected yearly profit and expected $6, the price was $180. If expected profit
drops to $5.50 and investors now pay only $30 again, the price becomes $165, an 8% fall, even
though the company is still growing.
