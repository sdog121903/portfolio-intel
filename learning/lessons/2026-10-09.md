# Lesson of the day, 2026-10-09

**Why did half your portfolio fall together on Thursday?**

On Thursday, chip and optical stocks fell in the morning (oil, high rates, profit-taking), and then a newspaper report said OpenAI's revenue pace was lower than people thought. Your 11 holdings tied to AI data centers held $121.98 on Wednesday, 53.5% of your money. On Thursday they lost **$5.86, or 4.8%**. Your other 6 holdings (Moderna, Agilent, CrowdStrike, Palo Alto, Snowflake and QQQM) lost **$0.60, or 0.57%**. The same worries hit the whole group at once.

The reason is **correlation**: how often two stocks move the same way on the same day. It runs from −1 to +1. At +1 they move in lockstep, at 0 they ignore each other. Lumentum and VIAVI have a 60-day correlation of 0.84. CrowdStrike and Palo Alto have 0.92. Agilent and your chip stocks have at most 0.22. Stocks are highly correlated when the same thing drives them: the same customers (the big AI spenders), the same budgets, the same worries.

**Worked example.** You own four optical stocks (Lumentum, AAOI, AXT and VIAVI), worth $45.81 on Wednesday. On Thursday they lost $3.54 together (−7.7%), and on Friday three of them bounced together. Four tickers, but on a day like Thursday they behaved like one bigger bet. Diversification depends on owning things that do *not* share the same driver, not on the number of tickers.

*Check yourself:* on Friday CrowdStrike rose 4.6% and Palo Alto 5.1%. If bad news hit cybersecurity next week, would owning both protect you much more than owning just one? Why? (Answer below.)

*Answer:* not much. With a correlation of 0.92, CrowdStrike and Palo Alto tend to fall on the same days by similar amounts, so together they behave like one bigger cybersecurity position (18.1% of your money, PANW 9.2% plus CRWD 8.9%), not two separate bets.
