# Lesson of the day, 2026-10-07

**Why can a small move in interest rates hit fast-growing stocks so hard?**

On Wednesday morning the interest rate on 10-year US government bonds jumped to about 5.36%, its highest since 2002, and your fastest-growing stocks opened lower. By the close the rate was back at 5.28%, almost where it started, yet many of them kept falling. So rates were at most part of Wednesday's story. But the mechanism is worth knowing, because it explains why your stocks react so strongly to rate news.

A share is a claim on all the profits a company will ever make. A dollar of profit that arrives years from now is worth less than a dollar today, because today's dollar could sit in a safe bond and earn interest in the meantime. So investors "discount" future profits: the higher the interest rate, the less a far-off dollar is worth today.

**Worked example.** What is $100 of profit worth today if investors demand 5.27% a year (Tuesday's 10-year rate) or 5.365% (Wednesday's morning peak)?

- Profit arriving in **1 year**: $94.99 vs $94.91. It loses 0.09%.
- Profit arriving in **20 years**: $35.80 vs $35.16. It loses 1.8%, about 20 times as much.

So the same small rise in rates hurts companies whose value depends on profits far in the future much more. Investors pay very high prices for CrowdStrike (about 209 times next year's expected earnings) or AXT because they expect big profits *later*. That makes those stocks more sensitive to rates. QQQM fell only 0.26%: much of it sits in giant companies that already earn large profits today (inference). (Real investors demand more than the bond rate for risky shares, so treat this as a simplified example. The direction is what matters.)

*Check yourself:* if the 10-year rate fell sharply tomorrow, which would you expect to rise more that day, all else equal: QQQM or CrowdStrike? Why? (Answer below.)

*Answer:* most likely CrowdStrike. More of its price rests on profits far in the future, so a lower discount rate lifts its value more, and it tends to move about 1.5 times as much as the market. "All else equal" matters: company news can always outweigh rates.
