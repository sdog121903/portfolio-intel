## Lesson of the day

**Why can a small move in interest rates hit fast-growing stocks so hard?**

On Wednesday morning the interest rate on 10-year US government bonds jumped to about 5.36%, its highest since 2002, and your fastest-growing stocks opened lower. By the close the rate was back at 5.28%, almost where it started, yet many of them kept falling. So rates were at most part of Wednesday's story. But the mechanism is worth knowing, because it explains why your stocks react so strongly to rate news.

A share is a claim on all the profits a company will ever make. A dollar of profit that arrives years from now is worth less than a dollar today, because today's dollar could sit in a safe bond and earn interest in the meantime. So investors "discount" future profits: the higher the interest rate, the less a far-off dollar is worth today.

**Worked example.** What is $100 of profit worth today if investors demand 5.27% a year (Tuesday's 10-year rate) or 5.365% (Wednesday's morning peak)?

- Profit arriving in **1 year**: $94.99 vs $94.91. It loses 0.09%.
- Profit arriving in **20 years**: $35.80 vs $35.16. It loses 1.8%, about 20 times as much.

So the same small rise in rates hurts companies whose value depends on profits far in the future much more. Investors pay very high prices for CrowdStrike (about 209 times next year's expected earnings) or AXT because they expect big profits *later*. That makes those stocks more sensitive to rates. QQQM fell only 0.26%: much of it sits in giant companies that already earn large profits today (inference). (Real investors demand more than the bond rate for risky shares, so treat this as a simplified example. The direction is what matters.)

*Check yourself:* if the 10-year rate fell sharply tomorrow, which would you expect to rise more that day, all else equal: QQQM or CrowdStrike? Why? (Answer at the end.)

## New words today

- **Present value / discounting**: what a future dollar is worth today. The higher interest rates are, the less a far-off dollar is worth now.
- **Profit-taking**: selling a stock that has risen fast, to lock in the gain. It can push a price down with no news.
- **Ex-dividend date**: the first day a buyer no longer gets the next dividend. The price usually drops by about the dividend that day, and owners before it still get the cash.
- **Proxy statement**: the papers a company sends before its yearly shareholder meeting, listing what owners vote on (directors, pay, auditor).
- **Form 3**: the first report a new insider files, listing the shares they already own.
- **Tax withholding (code F)**: shares a company keeps back to pay an employee's income tax when stock awards vest. Not a sale in the market.
- **10b5-1 plan**: a selling schedule an insider sets months in advance, so they do not choose the moment of each sale. Sales outside such a plan get more attention.
- **Expense ratio**: a fund's yearly fee as a share of your money. QQQM's 0.15% is 15 cents a year per $100.
- **Look-through (overlap)**: counting what is inside a fund. Your QQQM already holds AMD, Palo Alto and CrowdStrike, so you own a little more of them than your single positions show.
- **Optical circuit switch**: a switch that redirects light between fibres with tiny mirrors, without turning it into electricity. Like a railway switch for light.
- **Hybrid bonding**: joining stacked chips directly, copper to copper, used for AI memory.
- **Behind-the-meter power**: a power plant on the customer's own site, on their side of the electricity meter.
- **Convertible preferred shares**: special shares, usually sold to one investor, that can turn into ordinary shares later. If they do, the share count rises.
- **Convertible bond**: a loan that the lender can swap for shares above a set price (the conversion price).
- **Headline sensitivity**: a stock jumping on news about a theme (a disease scare, say) before anyone checks whether the company's sales would change.
- **CPU**: central processing unit, the general-purpose chip that runs a computer's software and hands work to other chips.
- **Endpoint**: any laptop, server or phone that connects to a company's network and must be protected.
- **Firewall**: a device or program that decides which network traffic is allowed in or out.
- **Overweight rating**: an analyst's view that a stock will do better than the market. It is an opinion, not a fact.
- **Phase 3 trial**: the last and largest round of testing in patients before a drug can be approved.
- **Priced in**: already reflected in the share price, because investors expected it. Then even good news may not lift the stock.
- **VaR (value at risk)**: a loss exceeded only on the worst 1 day in 20, based on past behaviour.
- **Valuation**: how much investors pay for each dollar of a company's sales or profit. CrowdStrike at about 209 times next year's expected earnings has a very high valuation; the S&P 500 is about 20 times.
- **Run rate**: today's sales pace stretched to a full quarter or year. It is a pace, not reported sales.
- **Vesting**: the moment shares promised to an employee become theirs to keep. Some are often kept back to pay tax (code F).
- **Minutes (of a central-bank meeting)**: the written record of what the Federal Reserve's policymakers discussed, published about three weeks later.
- **Extreme ultraviolet (EUV) machine**: ASML's giant printer that uses very short-wavelength light to draw the smallest circuit patterns onto chips.
