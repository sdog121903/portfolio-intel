## Lesson of the day

**You own 0.074 of a CrowdStrike share: what does that mean?**

A **share** is one equal slice of a company. Owning shares makes you a part-owner. When the company becomes more valuable, the price of each slice tends to rise, and when it pays out cash (a dividend), each slice gets the same amount.

A **fractional share** is a piece of one slice. Your broker records that you own, say, 0.074 of one CrowdStrike share. This is what let you spread $199.47 across 16 companies: one whole ASML share costs $1,859.86, more than nine times your whole portfolio.

A fraction behaves exactly like a whole share, just scaled down:

- **Same percentage return.** On Monday a whole CrowdStrike share rose 0.97%, from $270.04 to $272.67, a gain of $2.63. A 0.074 slice held through that day would have moved 0.074 × $2.63 ≈ $0.19. That is also 0.97%.
- **Same dividend per share, scaled down.** Agilent pays 25.5 cents per share. Your 0.03 shares would get 0.03 × 25.5 ≈ 0.77 cents.

So owning less than one share does not change how good or bad an investment is. It only changes how many dollars ride on it. That is why this report always shows both: the percentage (how the company did) and the dollars (what it meant for you).

## New words today

- **Fractional share**: a piece of one share. It earns the same percentage as a whole share, just on fewer dollars.
- **At-the-market (ATM) offering**: a company selling new shares a little at a time into the market through a broker, instead of in one big sale.
- **Selling overhang**: steady selling (here, the company's own new shares) that weighs on a price until it stops.
- **Schedule 13G**: the short form a large, passive holder (5% or more, not trying to influence the company) files with the SEC. A 13D is the long form for activists.
- **Index rebalancing**: index funds buying and selling on the day a company joins or leaves their list, usually at the closing price.
- **Treasury yield**: the interest rate the US government pays to borrow. When it rises, safe bonds compete harder with shares.
- **Interest-rate rise (rate hike)**: the central bank making borrowing more expensive to slow price rises. Investors usually treat it as bad for share prices.
- **Conversion price**: the share price above which a convertible bond's lender gains by swapping the loan for shares.
- **Presidential Symposium**: the main session of a big medical conference, kept for the most important new trial results.
- **Foundry**: a factory that manufactures chips designed by other companies.
- **Anchor customer**: a big first customer whose orders make a new factory worth building.
- **General Counsel**: a company's chief lawyer.
- **Settlement (T+1)**: when the shares and the cash actually change hands. In the US that is one business day after the trade.
- **Record date**: the day you must be on the company's books to receive a dividend.
- **Price target**: the price an analyst expects a stock to reach in about a year. It is an opinion, not a fact.
- **Remaining performance obligations**: contracts customers have signed but not yet been billed for, so future revenue already agreed.
- **VaR (value at risk)**: a loss exceeded only on the worst 1 day in 20, based on past behaviour.
- **Fiscal year (FY)**: a company's own accounting year, which need not match the calendar.
- **S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100**: lists (indexes) of large US companies. The S&P 500 holds about 500 and is used as "the market". The Nasdaq-100 holds 100 large, mostly tech, companies on the Nasdaq exchange. Index funds copy these lists.
- **52-week high**: the highest price of the past year.
- **Stop-loss and target**: prices you set in advance in columns S and T of your sheet. A stop-loss is where you would sell to limit a loss; a target is where you would take profit.
- **Phase 3 trial**: the last and largest round of testing in patients before a drug can be approved.
- **Insider**: a company's top managers and directors. They must report their own trades in its shares.
- **Source tiers**: how much this report trusts a source. Tier 1 = the company or a regulator said it. Tier 2 = top newsrooms. Tier 3 = useful, but to be confirmed.
- **Pillar, trigger and thesis status**: in your thesis files, a pillar is something that must stay true for your reason to own a stock, and a trigger is a warning sign you wrote down in advance. The status runs Intact (nothing important contradicts your reasons), Watch (a pillar is weaker or uncertain), Challenged (hard evidence contradicts a pillar) and Broken (a trigger has clearly happened).
